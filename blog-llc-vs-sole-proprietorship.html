LLC vs Sole Proprietorship for a Side Hustle: Which First?

By Ashley Murray / Ramlochun · Serial Entrepreneur · Hawaii Broker · 10+ Years in Finance

Here's a rule the wealthy operate by that most side-hustlers skip: the legal wrapper around your business is a decision, not a default. The moment you take your first payment for a service or product, you're already running a sole proprietorship whether you meant to or not — the IRS treats any unincorporated one-person business that way automatically. The question isn't whether you have a business structure. It's whether the one you have by default is the one you actually want.

Most people either overthink this (paying a lawyer $800 to form an LLC for a $200/month Etsy shop) or underthink it (running a six-figure consulting practice with zero liability protection). Here's the actual math and the actual risk, so you can make the call with real numbers instead of guesses.

The one-line difference that matters most

A sole proprietorship has no legal separation between you and the business. If the business owes money or gets sued, your personal savings, your car, and your home equity are all on the table. An LLC (limited liability company) creates a legal wall — creditors and lawsuits against the business generally can't reach your personal assets, only what the business itself owns. Everything else in this comparison is secondary to that one fact.

What each one costs to start — real numbers

A sole proprietorship costs $0 in federal filing fees. You just start operating under your own name and Social Security number. If you want to operate under a business name instead of your legal name, most states or counties require a DBA ("doing business as") or fictitious business name filing, which typically runs $10–$100 depending on where you file, sometimes with a renewal every 1–5 years.

An LLC requires a state filing fee for Articles of Organization. These vary widely:

Hawaii's own filing office (DCCA) confirms the $50 fee for Articles of Organization, plus a small state archives fee, with an optional $25 expedite. Most states also charge an ongoing fee to keep the LLC in good standing — Hawaii's annual report runs about $15; other states range from $0 to several hundred dollars a year (California's $800 annual franchise tax is the well-known outlier — check your own state before assuming Hawaii's low cost applies everywhere).

Run the math on a modest side hustle bringing in $3,000/month: an LLC costs roughly $50–$130 to form and $15–$75 a year to maintain in most states. That's under 1% of a single month's revenue for a legal shield that protects everything else you own.

Taxes: the part everyone gets wrong

Here's the detail that trips people up: forming an LLC does not change your tax bill by default. A single-member LLC is, for federal tax purposes, a "disregarded entity" — the IRS taxes it exactly like a sole proprietorship. Profit flows to your personal return on Schedule C, and once your net self-employment earnings clear $400 in a year, you owe self-employment tax at 15.3% (12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare) on top of ordinary income tax. The LLC wrapper changes your legal exposure — it does not lower what you owe the IRS unless you separately elect S-corp taxation, which is a different decision with its own payroll requirements.

Both structures qualify equally for the 20% Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction on eligible pass-through profit — a provision that was made a permanent part of the tax code under 2025 legislation, so it's not going away at year-end anymore. QBI has income phase-outs and rules that vary by profession, so this is exactly the kind of thing to run past your own CPA rather than assume applies the same way to every business.

The liability trade you're actually making

As a sole proprietor, there is no line between "the business's money" and "your money" — legally, they're the same pool. A client who slips on a rug during an in-home service call, a product that causes an allergic reaction, or a vendor contract you can't fulfill can all result in a judgment collected directly from your personal accounts.

An LLC breaks that connection — if you maintain it. The protection disappears if you commingle funds (paying personal bills from the business account), skip your state's annual filing, or personally guarantee a loan. Courts call this "piercing the corporate veil," and it's the single most common way people lose the protection they paid for. A separate business bank account, opened under your LLC's own EIN, is the minimum maintenance required to keep the wall standing.

So which one first?

Start as a sole proprietorship if you're testing an idea with genuinely low risk — freelance writing, tutoring, digital downloads, consulting with no in-person contact or physical product. You can operate today under your own SSN at zero cost and upgrade the moment revenue or risk increases.

Start with an LLC from day one if any of these apply: you have a physical product that could injure someone, you meet clients in person or in their homes, you sign contracts with real financial exposure, or you plan to hire anyone. At $50–$130 to form in most states, the LLC is cheap insurance against a single bad outcome that could otherwise reach your personal savings.

Once you've registered an entity and pulled a free EIN from the IRS, the next move most people skip is building a credit file under that EIN — which is a separate process from your personal credit and opens business funding your personal score never touches. Five mistakes that undo the protection

  1. Commingling funds. Paying personal expenses from the business account (or vice versa) is the fastest way to lose LLC liability protection in court.
  2. Assuming the LLC lowers your tax bill. By default it doesn't — the SE tax and income tax rules are identical to a sole proprietorship unless you elect S-corp status.
  3. Skipping the annual/biennial report. Miss it and your state can administratively dissolve your LLC — silently removing the protection you thought you had.
  4. Forming in another state for "better" fees. If you operate in Hawaii but form in Delaware or Wyoming, you'll likely have to register as a "foreign LLC" back home anyway — paying two states instead of one.
  5. Waiting too long to upgrade. The best time to form the LLC is before the contract that goes wrong, not after.

FAQ

Can I switch from a sole proprietorship to an LLC later?

Yes, and it's common. You form the LLC, get a new EIN under it, open a new business bank account, and move operations over. There's no penalty for starting as a sole proprietor and converting once the business proves itself.

Does an LLC protect me if I personally guarantee a loan?

No. If you sign a personal guarantee — common for a new business's first loan or lease — you're personally on the hook for that specific debt regardless of the LLC. The liability shield covers business obligations you haven't personally co-signed.

Do I need an LLC to get an EIN?

No. Sole proprietors can get a free EIN directly from the IRS too — useful if you want to open a business bank account or hire without using your personal SSN, even before forming an entity.

Is a single-member LLC required to file a separate tax return?

No, by default. A single-member LLC is a disregarded entity for federal tax purposes — its income and expenses are reported on your personal Schedule C, the same as a sole proprietorship, unless you elect corporate taxation.
